Traditional African medicine

Africa is a vast continent with thousands of medicinal plants, and much of what is in our homes came from it: frankincense, myrrh, gum arabic, hibiscus and coffee. Here is a look at traditional medicine there today, and what helps and what harms among its best-known remedies.

Before any traditional treatment

  • Fever after travelling to Africa, even weeks later: see a doctor immediately and tell them about your trip, as it may be malaria. Do not rely on herbs, and before travelling ask the doctor about prevention.
  • Snakebite: go to hospital immediately for antivenom, not to a traditional healer.
  • Do not abandon treatment for HIV, tuberculosis, diabetes or blood pressure for a herbal blend.
  • Do not use unknown roots or powders as enemas, and do not give them to children.
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women and children: no blends without the doctor's approval.

Traditional medicine in Africa

Traditional medicine in Africa today

World Health Organization

About 80% of Africa's population relies on traditional medicine for basic health needs. Today more than 40 African countries have a national policy regulating it, up from only 8 in 2000.

African Traditional Medicine Day

31 August, since 2003

An annual day marked by African countries with the World Health Organization, calling for the study of African herbs and the regulation of traditional healers.

Africa and the Arabs

Centuries of trade

Frankincense and myrrh came from the Horn of Africa, and gum arabic, hibiscus and baobab from Sudan. Coffee travelled from Abyssinia to Yemen and then to the world.

From South Africa to European pharmacies

Umckaloabo

The root of the African geranium became one of the best-known herbal cough medicines in Germany: an example of a traditional herb that entered research, though its evidence is still weak.

Sweet wormwood and malaria

The difference between a plant and a medicine

Artemisinin, the basis of modern malaria medicines, was extracted from this plant. But drinking the plant itself does not cure, because a medicine is a precise, pure dose and the plant is not.

Well-known African remedies: what helps and what harms

TreatmentIn AfricaWhat we know today
Shea butter for dry skin
Sound		From the shea tree of West Africa, rubbed on the body and hair.A good moisturiser for dry skin. Try it on a small patch first.
Rooibos, baobab, moringa and hibiscus
Sound		Everyday drinks and foods across Africa.Generally safe in food amounts. Hibiscus may lower blood pressure slightly, so take care if you take blood pressure medicine.
Gum arabic
Sound		From acacia trees in Sudan and the Sahel, dissolved in water.A soluble fibre that is gentle on the gut; drink it with enough water.
African geranium root «Umckaloabo»
With care		From South Africa, for cough and bronchitis.Cochrane review: it may ease symptoms of acute bronchitis, but the evidence is weak and all the studies were funded by the manufacturer. Rare cases of liver injury have been reported.
Devil's claw
With care		A root from the Kalahari desert, for joint and back pain.Cochrane review: it may ease back pain in the short term, but the evidence is weak. Avoid it if you have a stomach ulcer or take blood thinners.
African cherry bark «Pygeum»
With care		From African forests, for the prostate.Cochrane review of 18 studies: it may ease symptoms of an enlarged prostate, but the studies were small and short. Do not take it before a doctor examines the prostate to rule out cancer. Its trees are threatened by over-harvesting.
Sweet wormwood (Artemisia) tea for malaria
Harmful		An infusion drunk to prevent and treat malaria.The World Health Organization does not support using the plant in any form for malaria: the amount of active substance in it is insufficient and inconsistent, and it may spread drug resistance.
«Covid-Organics»
Harmful		A drink from Madagascar (2020) presented as a cure for COVID-19.The WHO Africa office said there was no evidence that it works.
Treating AIDS with African potato, garlic and beetroot
Harmful		Promoted by official bodies in South Africa instead of antiviral treatment.It does not treat the virus. A Harvard study (2008) estimated that delaying treatment in those years cost more than 330,000 lives.
Impila» root
Harmful		A root used in South Africa, including as enemas.Toxic to the liver and kidneys and lowers blood sugar. A medical thesis from Durban (1983) linked a pattern of liver death and kidney failure seen in 263 deaths to this root.
Iboga root
Harmful		A root used in rituals in Gabon; its extract is promoted as an addiction treatment.It disturbs the heart rhythm. 19 deaths were recorded outside Africa between 1990 and 2008, mostly in heart patients or with other drugs.
Yohimbe
Harmful		A bark from West Africa sold as a sexual stimulant.It raises blood pressure, speeds up the heart and causes anxiety, and is dangerous for heart patients.
Cutting the uvula in children
Harmful		A practice of some healers in East Africa for sore throat and vomiting.It causes bleeding and serious infections and delays treatment of the real illness, as a systematic review from Ethiopia (2024) documented.
