Perfumes and incense

Perfume is part of our lives and a beloved Sunnah, and incense is burned every week in most Gulf homes. Here: oud, musk, ambergris and frankincense, perfume in the Sunnah with the grade of each hadith, what science says about incense smoke in closed homes, and the safety of perfumes and essential oils, especially for children.

Emergency

  • A child who swallowed an essential oil, oud oil or camphor, even a little: do not make them vomit, and call an ambulance (998 in the UAE, or your local ambulance number if you are elsewhere) or a poison centre immediately.
  • Headache, dizziness and nausea in more than one person in a room with burning charcoal: get them into fresh air immediately, as it may be carbon monoxide poisoning.
  • Breathlessness or severe wheezing after incense in someone with asthma: move them away from the smoke and give their treatment; if they do not improve, go to the emergency department.

What are they?

Oud and oud oil

Wood from Aquilaria trees in Southeast Asia, which forms an aromatic resin when the wood is infected by a fungus. The wood is burned as incense, and oud oil is distilled from it. The natural kind is rare and expensive, the trees are endangered and protected internationally, and much of what is sold is imitation or wood soaked in synthetic oils.

Bakhoor, ma'moul and dukhoon

Blends made from oud chips, sandalwood, sugar, essential oils and musk, burned on charcoal or an electric burner. Their ingredients vary widely, and cheap ones are heavy in synthetic fragrance.

Frankincense and myrrh

Two resins from trees of southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa, known for thousands of years as incense and medicine. The guide has a page for each.

Musk

It originally came from a gland of the male musk deer, which is now protected, so most musk on the market is synthetic. «White musk» and «purity musk» sold in markets are mostly synthetic perfumes.

Ambergris

A substance formed in the gut of the sperm whale and washed ashore by the waves. It is very rare and expensive, and most of what is called «ambergris» in perfumes is a synthetic imitation scent.

Sandalwood, rose water and saffron

Sandalwood is a fragrant wood from India and Australia, rose water is distilled from Damask and Taif roses, and saffron is used in oriental perfumes. Rose water is the gentlest on the chest because it has no smoke.

Incense smoke and health

  • Relatively strong Incense smoke pollutes the air at home. In a study of the oud and bakhoor incense used in UAE homes, it released large amounts of fine particles, carbon monoxide, formaldehyde and nitrogen oxides, and caused inflammation in lung cells similar to the effect of cigarette smoke.
  • Relatively strong Asthma and allergy: in a study of children in Oman, those exposed to incense smoke were more likely to have asthma attacks. Incense irritates the nose, eyes, throat and skin, and triggers headaches.
  • Moderate Fine particles exceed the safe limit: in experiments in Bahrain in a room the size of a typical home room, fine particles exceeded the permitted limit in every test, with charcoal and with an electric burner.
  • Moderate Charcoal adds to the harm: it adds carbon monoxide. Burning incense in a closed room or closed car is dangerous, especially for anyone sleeping there.
  • Moderate Headaches and poor concentration: in a study of UAE homes, people who burned incense daily were two to four times more likely to complain of headaches, forgetfulness and poor concentration.
  • Limited Pregnancy and babies: studies suggest that exposure to incense may be linked to higher allergy markers in newborns. It is wiser to keep pregnant women and babies away from the smoke.

How to burn incense with less harm

  • Open a window or door during and after burning incense, turn on the extractor fan, and do not burn incense while the air conditioner recirculates the same air in a closed room.
  • Reduce the time and amount: a small piece for a few minutes is enough to perfume clothes, instead of hours on end.
  • Perfume clothes in another room then bring them in, not in the bedroom and not near children or people with asthma, allergies or heart disease.
  • An electric burner is better than charcoal because it adds no carbon monoxide, but it does not make the smoke safe.
  • Do not burn incense in a closed car, and do not sleep in a room with burning charcoal.
  • Do not spray alcohol-based perfume near a lit burner, as alcohol catches fire easily, and keep the burner away from curtains and children.
  • Smoke-free alternatives: rose water, airing the house, perfume on clothes, and fragrant flowers and plants such as basil and jasmine.

Perfumes and the skin

  • Moderate Fragrance allergy is one of the commonest causes of contact dermatitis: redness and itching where the perfume is applied. Try a new perfume on a small area first.
  • Moderate Dark patches after sun: some oils, such as bergamot and citrus oils, cause patches on the skin if it is exposed to the sun afterwards. Put perfume on clothes rather than the neck in the daytime.
  • Relatively strong Babies: no perfume or oud oil on their skin or near their face, as their skin is thin and their lungs are sensitive.
  • Limited Eczema and asthma: strong perfumes trigger both, so unscented products are better for those affected.

Essential oils

  • Limited Aromatherapy: inhaling lavender has trials for easing anxiety, and inhaling peppermint has trials for easing nausea, but the evidence is limited. It is a gentle aid, not a treatment.
  • Relatively strong Essential oils are poisonous if swallowed. Poisonings of children are increasing, mostly from accidental swallowing. Less than half a teaspoon going into the lungs can cause pneumonia.
  • Relatively strong The most dangerous for children: camphor oil causes seizures minutes after swallowing, eucalyptus oil causes severe drowsiness and can cause coma, and wintergreen oil contains a very high concentration of an aspirin-like substance. Clove, thyme and tea tree oils are also dangerous.
  • Relatively strong Keep them closed and away from children, and never put them in drink bottles. If a child swallows an essential oil, do not make them vomit; call an ambulance or a poison centre immediately.
  • Moderate On the skin: always dilute them in a plain oil, and do not apply them to the skin of babies and young children, or near their noses.

Common myths

«Incense purifies the air at home from germs»

Smoke adds pollutants to the air rather than purifying it. Ventilation is what cleans the air.

«Natural perfume does not cause allergy»

Natural oils are among the commonest causes of skin allergy; natural is not always safe.

«The Indian oud in the hadith is incense oud»

The Indian oud in the «seven cures» hadith is costus, a root used as medicine. Aloeswood (al-aluwwa) is the incense oud.

«Steaming the womb cleanses it and helps pregnancy»

There is no evidence for it, and it has caused burns. See the «Delayed pregnancy and fertility» section.

«Fumigating a sick child or baby with harmal or incense»

The smoke harms their lungs, and harmal is poisonous. If the child is ill, they need a doctor.

«Smelling essential oil cures colds and asthma»

It may give a feeling of an open nose, but it can trigger an asthma attack in some people, and its high concentration harms children.
