Extended guide

Where does it come from?

Farmers cut the bark of the tree after the rainy season, and sap flows out and hardens in the air into lumps that are collected weeks later. The trees grow in the «gum arabic belt» across the African Sahel from Senegal to Sudan and Eritrea. Before the war that began in 2023, Sudan exported about 70 to 80% of the world's hashab gum, and the finest comes from Kordofan.

History and heritage
  • Ancient Egypt: Acacia gum was used to mix paints and inks, and in mummification.
  • The name: Arab traders carried it from Africa through the ports of the Arabian Peninsula to Europe, so Europeans called it «gum arabic».
  • Arab medicine: The old Arab physicians mention it for a rough throat, cough and diarrhoea, because it coats the throat and intestines with a slippery layer.
  • In the Quran: Allah says: ﴿And talh trees layered with fruit﴾ [Al-Waqi'ah: 29]. Some commentators explained talh as the acacia tree known to the Arabs, while others said it is the banana.
  • In Sudan: «Gum water» (moyat al-samgh) is a home drink soaked overnight and drunk in the morning, and traditionally given to kidney patients.
  • In politics: When the United States imposed sanctions on Sudan in the 1990s, it exempted gum arabic, because soft drink and confectionery factories needed it.
In your food and medicines without your knowing

It is labelled «E414», «acacia gum» or «Gum arabic», and is used:

  • In soft drinks and juices, to keep the flavour oil mixed with the water.
  • In sweets, chewing gum, marshmallows and ice cream.
  • In tablets as a binder and coating.
  • In watercolour paints and inks, and it was once the glue on postage stamps.

The European Food Safety Authority reviewed its safety in 2017 and found no cause for concern at food levels.

What does the science say?
  • Moderate Food for the good gut bacteria: About 85 to 90% of it is soluble fibre that ferments in the colon. In a trial of 54 volunteers (2008), 10 grams a day for 4 weeks increased the good bacteria, and this was the best dose.
  • Limited Constipation and mild diarrhoea: Soluble fibre regulates the stool, but evidence for gum arabic alone is limited. In severe diarrhoea, oral rehydration solution remains the basis.
  • Limited Chronic kidney disease: Small trials, mostly in Sudan; a 2022 review found only 4 studies and pointed to lower urea and creatinine. Gut bacteria are thought to use up some urea while fermenting the gum. It does not replace dialysis or medicines.
  • Limited Sickle cell anaemia: In a Sudanese trial of 47 patients, 30 grams a day for 12 weeks slightly raised fetal haemoglobin (from 6.7% to 7.4%), with no comparison group.
  • Limited Cholesterol, blood sugar and weight: Few and conflicting results.
Usual amount
  • In studies: 10 to 30 grams a day dissolved in water.
  • Start with 1 teaspoon (about 5 grams) for a week, then increase gradually to 10 grams, which is enough for its effect on the gut.
  • Drink enough water with it.
  • Try it for 4 weeks, then judge its effect on you.
Safety
  • Generally safe in food amounts.
  • In the first week: Bloating, gas, nausea or soft stools in many people, usually easing within days. In the sickle cell trial, about a third of patients had mild symptoms that went away within a week.
  • Medicines: It reduces the absorption of some, and a Sudanese study found it reduces the absorption of the antibiotic amoxicillin. Take it 2 hours apart from any medicine.
  • Kidney patients: Only with the kidney doctor's approval, especially those whose fluid intake is restricted or who are on dialysis.
  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding and children: Food amounts only; therapeutic amounts have not been studied in them.
  • Allergy to it is rare, mostly asthma in people who breathe in its dust at work.
Buying and quality
  • Good hashab: Hard round lumps, from yellowish white to light amber, odourless, with a mild taste.
  • Soaked in cold water, it slowly dissolves into a clear, sticky liquid with no residue.
  • Beware of very dark lumps or lumps mixed with sand and bark, and powder of unknown origin that may be mixed with starch or sugar.
  • Buy from a licensed source, and keep it in an airtight container in a dry place.

Common uses

  • Soluble fibre that feeds the good gut bacteria (moderate evidence)
  • Constipation and mild diarrhoea (traditional use)
  • Soothing a rough throat and dry cough (Arab medical heritage)
  • Support for people with chronic kidney disease (small trials, under medical supervision)

Usual preparation

1 teaspoon to 1 tablespoon (5 to 10 grams) of the lumps or powder soaked overnight in 2 cups of water until dissolved, and drunk in the morning.

Cautions

  • Bloating, gas and nausea are common in the first week, so start with a small amount and increase gradually
  • Kidney patients should only start it with their doctor's approval, especially those whose fluid intake is restricted
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: food amounts only
  • Its dust may cause asthma in people who grind it or work in its processing

Possible drug interactions

  • It reduces the absorption of some medicines, including the antibiotic amoxicillin: take them 2 hours apart
